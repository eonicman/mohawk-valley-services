DIY Plumbing Guide for Mohawk Valley Homeowners

Living in the Mohawk Valley means dealing with some of the harshest winters in New York State. From frozen pipes in Gloversville to aging plumbing in Amsterdam's older homes, our region presents unique challenges. This guide covers the most common plumbing issues local homeowners face, with practical DIY fixes you can handle yourself.

Frozen Pipes — The #1 Winter Threat

When temperatures drop below zero in Montgomery and Fulton Counties, frozen pipes are the single most common plumbing emergency. Pipes along exterior walls in older Amsterdam and Johnstown homes are especially vulnerable.

How to prevent frozen pipes:

  • Insulate pipes in unheated areas: basements, crawl spaces, garages, and along exterior walls. Foam pipe insulation sleeves cost about $1 per foot at local hardware stores.
  • On bitter cold nights (below 10°F), let faucets drip slowly. Moving water is harder to freeze.
  • Open cabinet doors under kitchen and bathroom sinks to let warm air circulate around pipes.
  • If you have a crawlspace, seal any gaps where cold air blows directly on pipes.
  • Disconnect garden hoses and shut off outdoor spigots before the first freeze. Drain outdoor faucets completely.

If a pipe freezes:

  • Turn on the faucet so melting water can flow out as you thaw the pipe.
  • Apply heat with a hair dryer, heat gun on low setting, or warm towels. Work from the faucet toward the frozen section.
  • Never use an open flame (torch, lighter) to thaw pipes — this is a fire hazard and can damage the pipe.
  • If a pipe has burst, shut off the main water valve immediately and call a plumber.
Warning: If you see frost on a pipe, hear gurgling, or notice reduced water flow during a freeze, act fast. A frozen pipe that bursts can flood your home with hundreds of gallons per hour. Know where your main shut-off valve is before winter hits.

Fixing a Leaky Faucet

A dripping faucet wastes up to 3,000 gallons per year according to the EPA. In the Mohawk Valley, where many homes have older fixtures common to pre-1970 construction in Amsterdam and Johnstown, worn washers and O-rings are the usual culprits.

For compression faucets (two-handle):

  • Turn off the water under the sink.
  • Pop the cap off the handle and remove the screw underneath.
  • Lift off the handle and unscrew the stem with a wrench.
  • Replace the seat washer (usually a rubber disk at the bottom of the stem). Match the size at your local hardware store.
  • Reassemble in reverse order. Don't overtighten.

For cartridge faucets (single-handle, common in newer Gloversville homes):

  • Shut off water supply valves.
  • Remove the handle screw (often hidden under a decorative cap).
  • Pull the cartridge straight up and out.
  • Take the cartridge to the hardware store for an exact match — brands like Moen and Delta have many variations.
  • Insert the new cartridge, reassemble, and test.
Local tip: Security Plumbing and Heating Supply on North Perry Street in Johnstown carries a wide selection of replacement parts and can match most washers, cartridges, and O-rings. They're a great resource for hard-to-find parts for older fixtures.

Fixing a Running Toilet

A running toilet can waste 200+ gallons per day. The good news: most fixes cost under $15 and take 15 minutes.

Step-by-step diagnosis:

  • Flapper check: Remove the tank lid and add a few drops of food coloring to the tank water. Wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. Replace it (universal flappers cost about $5 at any hardware store).
  • Chain adjustment: The chain connecting the flapper to the handle should have about ½ inch of slack. Too tight and the flapper won't seal; too loose and it won't lift fully.
  • Fill valve: If water keeps rising above the overflow tube, adjust the float (the ball or cup on the fill valve). Lower it slightly so water stops about 1 inch below the overflow tube top.
  • Fill valve replacement: If adjusting doesn't work, replace the entire fill valve assembly. Universal kits run $10–15 and include clear instructions. Just shut off the water, flush to drain the tank, swap the part, and reconnect.
Quick test: If you're not sure which part is causing the problem, try this: after flushing, manually press the flapper down. If the running stops, it's the flapper. If water still fills above the overflow tube, it's the fill valve.

Water Heater Basics

Most Mohawk Valley homes use tank-style water heaters, and the hard water common in Fulton and Montgomery Counties means sediment buildup is a real concern. Here's how to keep your water heater running efficiently:

Annual maintenance — flush the tank:

  • Turn off the power (electric) or set to "pilot" (gas). Connect a garden hose to the drain valve at the bottom of the tank.
  • Run the hose to a floor drain or outside. Open the valve and let 2–3 gallons drain to flush sediment.
  • If water is very dirty, drain the entire tank: shut the cold water inlet, open a hot faucet somewhere in the house (for air), and drain fully. Then refill before restoring power.

Temperature setting: Set your water heater to 120°F. Every 10° above that costs roughly 3–5% more in energy. In our cold-climate region, 120° is plenty for showers and prevents scalding.

When to replace: Most tank water heaters last 8–12 years. Check the serial number — the first two digits are usually the year of manufacture. If yours is over 10 years, budget for replacement before it fails.

Safety note: Never turn the temperature above 140°F. This creates a scalding hazard and accelerates tank corrosion. If your hot water smells like rotten eggs, that's bacteria in the sediment — flush the tank and consider replacing the anode rod.

Other Common Mohawk Valley Plumbing Quick Fixes

Slow drains: Try boiling water first (especially effective on soap-scum clogs common with hard water). For tougher clogs, use a plunger or a hand-crank drain snake. Avoid chemical drain cleaners — they damage older pipes common in our region.

Garbage disposal jammed: Press the red reset button on the bottom. If that doesn't work, use the Allen key that came with the unit in the bottom center hole to manually free the blades.

Outdoor spigot leaking in spring: If you notice dripping after turning the water back on, the freeze-proof hose bib may have cracked over winter. This requires replacing the fixture — a straightforward job if you have basic plumbing skills, or a quick call to a local plumber.
